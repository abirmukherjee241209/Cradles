Introduction

This handout is a practical introduction to Lagrangian mechanics, especially aimed at olympiad-style physics problem solving. Rather than focusing on complete mathematical rigor, the emphasis is on developing intuition and computational skill.

Basic Definitions

Lagrangian

The Lagrangian of a system is defined by:

\[ L = T - V \]

where \(T\) is the kinetic energy and \(V\) is the potential energy.

Generalized Coordinates

A generalized coordinate describes the configuration of an entire system. Examples include translational coordinates like \(x\) and angular coordinates like \(\theta\).

\[ q = \begin{pmatrix} q_1 \\ q_2 \\ \vdots \\ q_n \end{pmatrix} \]

Action

The action of a system along a path \(q(t)\) is:

\[ S = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} L(q_i,\dot q_i,t)\,dt \]

Euler–Lagrange Equation

The Euler–Lagrange equation is the fundamental equation of motion in Lagrangian mechanics.

\[ \frac{d}{dt} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q} \right) = \frac{\partial L}{\partial q} \]
Unlike Newtonian mechanics, the Euler–Lagrange equations remain valid in arbitrary coordinate systems and frames.

Coupled Oscillators

Systems with multiple degrees of freedom often produce coupled differential equations.

\[ M\ddot X = -KX \]

The eigenfrequencies satisfy:

\[ \det(M^{-1}K - \omega^2 I) = 0 \]

Worked Example

Oscillating Cylinder Inside a Hollow Cylinder

A solid cylinder of mass \(m\) and radius \(r\) oscillates inside a hollow cylinder of radius \(R\).

Using the rolling constraint:

\[ (R-r)\theta = r\phi \]

The Lagrangian becomes:

\[ L = \frac{3}{4}m(R-r)^2\dot\theta^2 + mg(R-r)\cos\theta \]

Applying the Euler–Lagrange equation and using the small angle approximation gives:

\[ \omega = \sqrt{ \frac{2g}{3(R-r)} } \]

When to Use Lagrangian Mechanics

  • Systems with many constraints
  • Multiple degrees of freedom
  • Problems involving generalized coordinates
  • Oscillations and coupled systems
  • Situations where Newtonian force analysis becomes messy

Selected Problems

  2. Dynamics of a rope over a pulley
  3. Acceleration of wedges with constraints
  4. Coupled oscillator systems
  5. Eigenfrequencies in LC circuits

Hamilton’s Principle

Hamilton’s Principle states that the physical path taken by a system is the one for which the action is stationary.

\[ \delta S = 0 \]

This principle unifies mechanics into a variational framework and forms the foundation of modern theoretical physics.
